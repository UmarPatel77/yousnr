Chapter 1: Chapter 1: The Early History

0:00September, 1994, Nintendo of Japan. Development of Super Mario 64 begins. From the very beginning, 0:07a major design decision was made: while the transition to 3D did open up many exciting possibilities, at the center of it all would be Mario’s most basic and iconic ability: his jump. 0:18So that’s what they did, and they did it right. In 1996, when Super Mario 64 came out, it was an immediate success. At the core of 0:27the tightest and most responsive controls ever seen in a 3D game was a wide range of unique abilities accessible at the press of the A button: single jumps, double jumps, 0:36triple jumps, triple jumps but with sparkles, sideflips, long jumps, wall kicks, backflips, jump kicks, jump dives! So, naturally, players thought: what if we didn’t use any of them? 1:17The late 90s, early 2000s Internet was a magical place, like the kingdom far away in a fairytale. Nearly all of it was either modernized or abandoned, but every now and then, 1:27you find an artifact of a simpler time preserved in amber, seemingly for generations to come. One 1:33of these is Walton Dell’s website, where all sorts of Nintendo 64 tricks and findings were 1:38reported by Walton Dell himself, or submitted by email. Some were glitches, some were fun little challenges you could try and complete, and some were downright stupid, like imagining a face in 1:48the ground texture, or telling you to “track down all 12 Viking bombs”. Until October 16, 1:542000, when an anonymous visitor sent this long email, blissfully unaware of the consequences 2:00that would unfold over the next two decades. Among many others, there was something he called the A Button challenge: try to get every star in Bob-Omb Battlefield while using A as little as possible. 2:11Even though the email was pretty clear about it, for some reason, Walton Dell was confused as to whether to count the cannon shots as A presses. He figured that Shoot to the Island in the Sky, 2:22which was submitted in 1 A press, was impossible if you counted cannon shots, and so he decided not to count them, in which case the star became trivial in 0 presses. 2:30But in the email, the strategy for it was actually explained in detail. Simply run to this bridge and fall off, doing a ground pound to break the wing cap 2:39box and get the wing cap. Afterwards, use this cannon to shoot up, and while flying, ground pound the star box, breaking it and getting the star in a single A press to shoot the cannon. 2:49Interestingly, the email mentions a guy named Miles, and the star is listed on Walton Dell’s 2:55website as “Alex’s challenge”. But who was that mysterious visitor? Well, it turns out that it was the legendary Alex Penev, one of the most prolific Nintendo 64 players of the time, 3:05who set hundreds of world records in Mario Kart 64 back in the game’s first decade, and is still active today in the Mario Kart community. In 1999, a part of the community was using IRC to discuss 3:16shortcuts and strategies. Although Mario Kart 64 was the primary focus, other games sometimes 3:21came up. Together with Myles Bukrim, Jason Walls and David Wonn, who also had his own website of N64 tricks and glitches, Penev and friends came up with a number of challenges, such as trying to get as many stars coinless, capless and cannonless, and, of course, the A Button challenge. 3:35Alex’s email was surprisingly detailed for what actually ended up on Walton Dell’s website. Many 3:42more A Button Challenges were submitted with strategies to solve them that were way ahead of their time. For example, in Cool Cool Mountain, the red coin star was already known to be possible 3:51in 0 A presses. This coin is particularly tricky, and the strategy to get it, found by Jason Whalls, 3:56was surprisingly clever: first, touch the chairlift at the bottom to make it go up. Then, quickly run to the warp to take it up, and drop down to the coin just in time 4:05to dive and land on the chairlift. Or, again in Cool Cool Mountain, Wall Kicks Will Work. The strategy here was to reach this spindrift, then use A to jump on its head and hold the button, 4:15never releasing it. When spinning in the air, Mario will twirl faster and fall slower when A is held. Using that slower fall technique, it's possible to reach the star with only one A press. 4:25It’s unclear if it was before or after, but the A Button challenge was also submitted by a visitor named Nathan for the red coin star in Snowman’s Land. Here, 4:33Dell said that it was easy once you got to the Koopa shell, but he couldn’t find a way to reach the shell in the first place, so he labeled the challenge impossible. However, in his email, 4:41Penev detailed a strategy to reach the star Twirl From the Freezing Pond with 0 A presses, so it’s very likely that Nathan had done the same thing for the red coin star. 4:49For reasons known only to himself, Walton Dell never released any more of the numerous A Button Challenges that Penev included in his email. For a whole year, this 4:59disappointment was the only documentation of the A Button Challenge available on the Internet. But that changed in 2001, when the very first web page dedicated to the challenge 5:08was created by GameFAQs user Chris Oldfield, also known as humantree. He had a list of his own best scores for most stars in the first levels of the game. For some of them, 5:18Alex Penev had already done better - together, they exchanged strategies on ICQ for a short while. But since the very beginning, something had been lacking. The A button challenge had 5:28always been considered on an individual star basis - even the 100-coin star was tackled independently 5:33from other stars. Humantree was the first to break those rigid rules when he came up with this idea: how many stars can you collect from a fresh file, without touching the A button once? 5:43He gave it his best, using all the strategies known at the time. The final result was 10 stars. 5:49Unfortunately, Bowser in the Dark World required the A button to be completed, so humantree was locked out of most of the castle. Also, Big Boo’s Haunt is only unlocked at 12 stars, 5:58so even that level was just barely out of the question. So, being limited to only four levels, he collected Big Bob-omb on the Summit, Footrace with Koopa the Quick, Shoot into the Wild Blue, 6:07every star in Cool, Cool, Mountain except for Wall Kicks Will Work, and the Princess’s Secret Slide in under 21 seconds. Of course, no video of this feat remains today. 6:15Humantree’s page eventually went offline, and no archive of it can be found. That piece of history is on the brink of being forever lost, 6:23only existing in the memory of maybe a handful of people. And then, that was it. After figuring out about 30 stars, and getting 10 stars with 0 A presses from a fresh file, 6:34nobody extended the challenge to the whole game. Nobody asked the crucial question: How many A presses does it take to beat Super Mario 64? For years, that question laid dormant. 6:45In the meantime though, more resources for the game started to pop up. In November of 2002, another regular GameFAQs user, a high school student by the name of Curtis Bright, 6:55made a website that eventually became sm64.com, which is still online to this day. The site had 7:00a comprehensive list of every coin, 1-up, warp and other secret in the game. It also had the 7:06world records for timed challenges with videos, which, at the time, was unheard of. Holding most of these world records were a couple of guys. Their names? Myles Bukrim and Alex Penev. 7:17One day in 2003, Penev sent Bright a message over AIM with the extension of the original Cool, Cool, Mountain A Button challenge to include the whole stage in a single A press. This piqued 7:28Bright’s interest enough that he tried the same thing for Bob-Omb Battlefield, Whomp’s Fortress, Jolly Roger Bay, Big Boo’s Haunt, Lethal Lava Land and Tall Tall Mountain as well. The result was 45 7:38different stars in 48 A presses. He eventually created a page on his website for the A Button 7:43challenge where he posted his scores, at some point in late 2005. But he was alone. For years, the A Button Challenge was all but dead, until this fateful day: July 13th, 2007. 7:57On the GameFAQs forums, a user by the name of Kirbymuncher created a new thread for the A Button Challenge. Curtis Bright was one of the first to participate in this thread, 8:06and he mentioned that another player from Brazil had set up a records page of his own, and that he had done even more stars. His name was Thiago Trujillo. 8:13It turns out that Thiago had done a lot more stars. In fact, two months prior to the GameFAQs thread, he’d already done 112 stars in 244 A presses. 8:23By this time, Nintendo 64 emulation was getting quite popular, and some people achieved stars in 8:28fewer A presses than ever before using savestates for the hardest parts. Bright noted that he should make a world record list, separating real-time scores from tool-assisted ones for fairness. One 8:39of those TASers was bobmario511. He came up with creative strategies to lower the A press count of many different stars, but perhaps his most impressive achievement is posting videos of them 8:49that are still available 14 years later. In fact, he posted over a hundred of them. At this time, the few stars that were still missing were mostly in Tick Tock Clock and Rainbow Ride, 8:58as well as Bowser in the Sky and the Dire Dire Docks 100 coin star, because they were deemed too daunting and couldn’t be done in fewer than at least 10 A presses. 9:06In the meantime, Curtis Bright had followed through. He updated his website with a leaderboard, divided between real-time and tool-assisted runs, where people could submit 9:15their own scores with videos. Among those people were Bright himself, bobmario511 and Thiago Trujillo, but also many unknown players like EnNopp112, SilentSlayers, sonicpacker and 9:25Robert Z, to name a few. It’s a shame none of those guys ever did anything else of significance. 9:31The earliest date with an A press count for the whole game is on Curtis Bright’s website, 9:35on June 12th, 2008. All 120 stars were collected with a grand total of 217 A presses. But there 9:43are two major caveats to this count. First, the good news: every star was counted in isolation. 9:49And now, the bad news: every star was counted in isolation. Because the 100 coin star doesn’t kick Mario out of the stage, when collected together with another star in the same stage, 9:58they can combine for fewer A presses than when collected separately. We can reasonably estimate that about 12 to 15 redundant A presses could be taken off the total. However, 10:08at the time, a full 120-star count wasn’t being considered, and with incomplete data to analyze, it’s impossible to get a clear number without large leaps and drawing conclusions. So, 10:18we’ll keep those redundant A presses in for the time being. But this number doesn’t account for the A presses needed to navigate the castle and enter every stage. The Secret Aquarium, 10:28Hazy Maze Cave and the Metal Cap course require one A press each to access. Rainbow Ride, Wing Mario Over the Rainbow, the Vanish cap course and Bowser in the Sky all require 10:37two. And finally, Tiny-Huge Island and Tick Tock Clock require one for every single stage entry, for a total of 23 additional A presses, which brings the earliest full count to 240. 10:48During the month of June 2008, another TASer by the name of alkhdaniel managed to squeeze out another 6 A presses out of Tick Tock Clock, 10:56which brought the total down to 234. His videos went largely unnoticed, like this improvement on the 100-coin star getting one single comment from a clueless gamer. 11:05With a submission-based leaderboard, a GameFAQs thread bursting with activity, the advent of tool-assisted runs, and every single star finally having an A press count, 11:14the A Button Challenge entered its golden age. The quest to lower the total number of A presses to beat Super Mario 64 with all 120 stars had officially begun. Immediately, the progress that was made was nothing short of incredible. 11:38Curtis Bright’s page never really caught on. It got submissions from about 30 different people over the next 3 years, but no one actually improved any scores on there. 11:49The GameFAQs thread died out after a few months and 300 posts. Instead of lowering the count, players simply assumed they had solved it. 11:58It took three years for any improvement to come. But when it did, it came from an unexpected source, and it was glorious. 12:2420 stars in no A presses. Again, stunted by Bowser in the Dark World blocking access to 12:37most of the castle. This tool-assisted run was made by a group of Japanese TASers in early 12:422011 and published to YouTube on March 5th, where it made quite the splash: 78 12:48thousand views in only 6 days. Articles were written about it. Unfortunately, little is known about the development of the TAS, since the only contributors that are still 12:56active today don’t remember much about it. But it did inspire another Japanese TASer to find an improvement in Jolly Roger Bay’s Plunder in the Sunken Ship only two days later, which allowed both that star and the next to be collected from a fresh file, bringing the total to 22. 13:11And, yet again, that was it. The challenge was largely forgotten for nearly two and a half years. 13:18But this dead period turned out to be very special: it would be the last one. On August 25th, 2013, an unlikely figure reappeared. Remember this 13:29clueless gamer from five years ago? This is pannenkoek2012. 13:35What he would achieve in the next few years is so revolutionary, so absolutely incredible that it singlehandedly elevated the A button challenge to heights that no one could have possibly imagined. 14:00Here’s every A press minimally required to collect all 120 stars in Super Mario 64. That’s 208 of 14:08them. Some stages like most of the early ones, are already pretty empty. But the later stages, especially the very vertical Tick Tock Clock, are a total nightmare. But this is about to change. A 14:18revolution is about to happen. In just two years from now, nearly all of these will be gone.

Chapter 2: Chapter 2: The Pannenkoek Revolution

14:29Pannenkoek2012. Originally starting out just watching videos about the game, he started making his own mark in 2010 by posting videos of getting all the coins 14:39available in every stage. But for some of them, he posted the maximum possible score of 255, 14:45way above the actual coin count. He pulled that off using a complex technique that even he didn’t fully understand at the time. Unfortunately, the videos are pretty low quality: 14:54they were done with some free trial of a screen capture software recording Project 64. It could have been worse: it could have been some hot garbage like we’ve seen before. 15:03By 2013, he felt like he had figured out the maximum coin count for every stage, so he moved 15:09on into a new direction. He began to investigate all sorts of glitches, sharing his findings in a couple dozen videos, until he felt like he had accumulated enough knowledge to tackle the A 15:19Button Challenge. On August 25th, he began a long journey through every single star in the game one 15:24by one, by posting an A Button Challenge video for each and every one of them. Among the very first 15:30ones he did were two Bob-omb Battlefield stars, Shoot to the Island in the Sky and Find the 8 red coins. The main issue with those stars is needing to reach the floating island without using A, 15:40and the strategy he came up with was absolutely groundbreaking. While everyone else had been playing checkers, Pannenkoek busted out the 4D chess. This was the first stroke of a genius 15:51so legendary that it would later earn him his own Wikipedia page. This is the art of cloning. 16:01It’s only natural that Pannenkoek’s first major technical breakthrough came through the use of a glitch that he spent years mastering with maximum coin scores. Before 16:10we delve into how this magic trick works, we need to understand the cloning glitch and its underlying game mechanics. Buckle up because it’s time for a game physics lesson. 16:22Okay, a word of warning here: this is going to be very long, but I do not recommend skipping any of it. Cloning is to this day possibly the single most capital technique of this challenge, 16:31and skipping its explanation could leave you pretty clueless for a sizeable chunk of this history series. So take out your textbooks and let’s get started. 16:42Before we get to it though, I need to point out that Pannenkoek has already covered this subject 16:49in scrupulous detail before. In fact, pretty much all the information on game mechanics that I will ever tell you comes directly from his past work. Look out in the bottom 16:58right corner of your screen for numbered notes pointing to additional information or clarifications. You can find a list of all the notes in the video description, 17:06as well as a glossary of the technical terms. There will also sometimes be timestamps to earlier points in the video if you need a refresher on a given topic. 17:13Cloning is made possible thanks to Mario’s ability to grab objects. When Mario grabs an object, what you see in his hands is actually a visual copy of the object he’s holding. The real 17:24object is made invisible and usually intangible until Mario releases it. We’ll call these the 17:29original object and the held object. Because the original object still exists and is still loaded, but it simply cannot be interacted with, we refer to it as being in limbo. Its animation 17:39data is still being updated, but it’s no longer being rendered. Meanwhile, the held object is an illusion cobbled together from three different sources: its position is tracked independently, 17:49its rotation is copied over from Mario’s rotation, and its animation is copied from the original object. What happens with the original object’s position depends on what type 17:58of object it is. Some stay in place, and some follow Mario around at or near his position. 18:03An interesting case to look at is the bob-omb. Its fuse is lit when Mario grabs it, 18:09which causes it to start smoking. Because the held object is just a visual copy, it’s not capable of 18:15generating smoke particles. The original object is kept close to where the visual copy is so that the smoke particles coming out of the invisible bob-omb appear to be coming out of the bob-omb 18:24Mario is holding. Additionally, bob-ombs are an exception in that they stay tangible when held. And that is the root cause of the bloated bob-omb glitch. For a few frames before it explodes, 18:35a bob-omb dramatically increases in size. If Mario grabs it at the right moment, he can hold it in its bloated state. Mario is then pushed back by the collision with the invisible bloated bob-omb, and it will keep following him around so long as he’s holding it. 18:49But what happens if Mario grabs the bob-omb while it’s exploding? Well, this is where it gets fun. 18:54But before we get there, we need to understand how grabbing and releasing an object works. When 19:00Mario grabs an object, multiple things need to happen. First, the object is established as the target of the grab. Then, Mario’s state needs to be changed to holding an object, and the object 19:11itself needs to change its state to being held, which turns it invisible and intangible - again, most of the time. For this whole process to function properly, Mario and the original object need to be linked somehow, otherwise Mario could never retrieve an object in limbo. 19:24To achieve this, the game uses a very simple system. Every object that is loaded is assigned 19:30the first available slot of 240 object slots. While in reality, object slots have addresses that look a bit like this, for simplicity’s sake, let’s instead give them a number which 19:40ranges from 1 to 240. When Mario grabs an object, the object itself is put in limbo, 19:45and Mario is given a reference number that links him back to it. That reference is the held object, or that visual copy of the original object. The only information the held object needs to have 19:55is the number of the slot the original object belongs to. So long as the object is being held, it will stay loaded in limbo. When Mario releases the held object by throwing or dropping it, the game looks at the reference number, and it brings back the associated original object from limbo. 20:10But remember, the original object’s position in limbo doesn’t reflect the position of the held object while Mario is carrying it around. When the object is brought back, its position needs to be 20:20updated to reflect the held object’s last position and complete the illusion. That position is tracked as a set of XYZ coordinates conveniently called the held object’s last position, or the 20:30HOLP for short. While Mario is holding an object, the HOLP is constantly updated to match the position of Mario’s hands, and as soon as Mario releases it, the original object is instantly 20:39warped to the HOLP as the held object disappears. There’s one small but key detail: to help create 20:45a seamless transition, the original object isn’t placed exactly at the HOLP depending on Mario’s animation. When Mario is dropping an object to the ground, the game aligns it with the HOLP horizontally, but places it at Mario’s vertical position, which sets it down on the ground. 20:58Most of the time, this entire process is seamless and goes exactly according to plan. But it’s not 21:04airtight, and the tiny cracks in it are just enough to let a clever and meticulous player cause a complete meltdown. Remember that when an object is being grabbed, both Mario and the object 21:14change their state independently from each other, and are linked together by the held object. In the case of a diving grab or a water grab, all of this happens at the same time. But for a punching grab, 21:25the signal to change states is established two frames before the actual state change. This gap can be abused if the original object unloads during those two frames. 21:34There are multiple ways to cause that to happen. The most common are to let a cork box sit still for 30 seconds and to grab it in the final two frames before it disappears, 21:43or to grab a bob-omb as it’s exploding. Anything that causes an object to unload within two frames of Mario grabbing it with a punch grab will work, but it’s also achievable by touching a loading zone, a trigger that unloads part of a level and loads another part in its place. 21:57Unloaded objects can no longer be updated, which means that the original object does not go into limbo. However, Mario still receives a reference for it in the form of the held object, 22:07but that held object is now linked to a vacant object slot, or a slot that is ready to load any new object in it. A vacant slot is marked as empty and its contents can 22:16no longer be modified. As a result, the held object looks invisible or frozen, since it represents the final state of the original object before it unloaded. 22:25When a new object is loaded in that slot, the reference now points to a completely different object than what was originally intended. While it appears like we’ve cloned the new object, 22:34this is not true yet. Mario is merely holding a reference to the new object, and because that object is not in limbo, 22:41it looks like there are two of them at once. However, when Mario throws what he’s holding, you can see that the copy disappears and the original object is warped near Mario’s position. 22:51But what happens if the new object cannot be thrown, like a goomba? Each grabbable object has a script that takes care of its behavior when grabbed, dropped and thrown. If the new 23:01object is missing that script, it will simply do nothing when thrown. Its behaviour script will essentially be erased and it won’t be able to function normally anymore, so it’ll stay frozen in 23:11place. This has a very important side-effect when the object being cloned has a spawner. Spawners are put in place to handle the loading and unloading of a group of objects, 23:21like a coin line, a coin ring, or a goomba trio, for example. When Mario moves far enough away from the broken goomba, it remains loaded, yet its spawner doesn’t get the memo, 23:30and so it also keeps working just the same. So, the goomba can be spawned again even though a broken copy of it already exists, and it has successfully been cloned. 23:40Because the process of cloning involves erasing the behaviour of the object being cloned, it suffers from inherent limitations. First, the object being cloned needs to have a spawner, 23:50otherwise it can only be transported. That can still be useful though, and in fact, it is still referred to as cloning even if the object in question isn’t actually duplicated. But the most 24:00important limitation is that a clone cannot move or behave anything like the object it’s supposed to be. Luckily, the interactions that Mario can have with an object aren’t handled directly by 24:10the behaviour script, so clones can be interacted with. Unfortunately, the missing behaviour script is responsible for handling the interaction status of the object, so after a single interaction, 24:20it also malfunctions. So while Mario can interact with a clone, he can only do it once. For example, 24:27throwing a coin clone causes Mario to collect it, but leaves it frozen in place and impossible to collect again. Throwing a goomba clone causes Mario to immediately be knocked back by it, after which it becomes frozen and intangible, essentially making the clone utterly useless. 24:42Fortunately, there is a workaround. If Mario throws a goomba clone while he’s falling fast enough, Mario will move out of the way as he releases the clone and 24:51they will not collide. So, Mario can come back and bounce on it once, and only once. 24:56So, all Pannenkoek had to do was to clone a goomba and throw it while falling, then come 25:01back to bounce on it and reach the island, right? Wrong. There’s one more major problem. To release 25:07an object clone, the object being cloned has to be close enough to still be loaded. Unlike objects in limbo, which are forced to stay loaded for as long as Mario is holding them, 25:16cloned objects aren’t bound by this rule, so they unload when Mario goes outside a certain radius from their spawners. While over here, the goomba trio under the island can be loaded, 25:26over there, they no longer can. This called for another trick from the bag: remote release. 25:32What seems like two hours ago, I said that the held object’s last position is constantly updated as Mario walks around holding something. It turns out that there’s a 25:41way to prevent it from being updated. There are a few ways to do this, but for now, we’re going to look at the Hat in Hand glitch. The animation for putting on a hat has two stages: 25:50during the first stage, Mario is holding the hat in his hand, and in the second stage, the hat is on his head. When Mario picks up a hat, the first stage is triggered: Mario starts 26:00holding the hat, and the animation plays. Now, if Mario comes across a second hat to put on, as soon as the first animation is over, a second animation plays, but the animation 26:09timer doesn’t reset. As a result, the second animation is instantly interrupted and Mario ends up with his hat in his hand. Putting on two hats in quick succession can happen either 26:18by duplicating hats using a warp or by reusing a special cap box before the first one expires. 26:24This glitch has important ramifications regarding the HOLP. If Mario grabs an object while holding 26:30a hat, the object will still be held like normal, but Mario will look like he’s holding his hat instead. Because the held object isn’t being rendered, the HOLP is not updated like it 26:39should. This causes the HOLP to become disjointed from Mario, because the position remains frozen 26:44to wherever it was last updated. For example, if Mario releases a cork box here, then performs the 26:51hat in hand glitch and throws a bob-omb, the bob-omb will be released where Mario released the cork box earlier, because that’s where the Hope still is. This is known as remote release. 27:01Despite the complex setup and many drawbacks, this is how Pannenkoek managed to get onto the island in Bob-omb Battlefield without using A. First, he grabbed an exploding bob-omb, 27:11which gave him a reference to a vacant object slot. Then, he carried it to this gap where he released it to place the HOLP in mid-air. Next, he did the Hat in Hand glitch and used another 27:21bob-omb to clone a goomba from this trio. Using remote release, he transported that goomba over 27:26to the HOLP, where he could go and bounce on it. Just one goomba wasn’t enough - he needed more to make a bridge to the island. But here’s the catch - to place a goomba further along, 27:36the HOLP needs to be further along, which means Mario himself needs to be further along, so he needs to bounce on this goomba to place that goomba. But remember, clones can only be 27:46interacted with once, so once Mario bounces on the first goomba to place the second goomba, the first goomba becomes intangible. So, after placing the second goomba, 27:55Mario needs to come back and place a third goomba next to the first one so he can bounce on these two. This process becomes more tedious the longer the gap is, and exponentially so. 28:04You see, it’s sort of like counting in binary, except the 1s are goombas that you place, and the 0s are gaps that need to be filled. The length of the bridge is the amount of 1s 28:13that you need in a row. Therefore, for a bridge of length 1, you need a single goomba. For length 2, 28:19you need 3 goombas, length 3, 7 goombas, length 4, 15 goombas, and for a bridge of length n, 28:25you need 2 to the power of n minus 1. This can rapidly become a problem, because the game crashes from having to draw too many triangles if there are somewhere 28:34around 40 goombas on screen. That puts the upper bound of a goomba bridge’s length at only 5. 28:39In this case, Pannen was able to get across using three goomba bounces, which means he had to place 7 goomba clones to cross the gap. For the red coins, 28:48this was sufficient, but for this star, he needed something extra to reach the star. A goomba to bounce on would be nice. There are two options to get that to happen. The 28:57first is to keep the same strategy: set up the Hope under the star, then remote release a goomba clone from elsewhere and reap the benefits. There’s one major issue, though: 29:06because the wing cap box on the island doesn’t allow for the Hat in Hand glitch to be possible, the cloning has to be done off the island after the Hope was put on there first. A goomba bridge is a single use infrastructure, so two trips to the island require two separate goomba bridges. 29:21The second option is to release the clone directly at the location. Fortunately, the top of the island is close enough to these goombas that they can be loaded, 29:29and therefore, cloned, while on the island. Unfortunately, Mario can’t release the clone without instantly bumping into it and ruining its purpose, 29:37because there’s no way to release it by falling from above.
